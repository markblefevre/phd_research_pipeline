## Research question
What does the paper ask?

What are the main ways large language models can be applied in finance, and how should practitioners choose among zero-shot/few-shot use of general-purpose models, fine-tuning, tool augmentation, and training finance-specific LLMs from scratch? The paper also asks what tradeoffs in data, computation, cost, performance, privacy, and risk should guide adoption.

## Data
This is a survey rather than an original empirical study, so it does not construct a primary dataset.

The paper synthesizes results reported in prior work on financial LLMs and related financial AI applications. It reviews general-purpose and finance-specific models including GPT-style models, LLaMA, BLOOM, FinMA/PIXIU, FinGPT, Instruct-FinGPT, Fin-LLaMA, BloombergGPT, XuanYuan 2.0, and Fin-T5.

It also summarizes reported training-data sizes, compute requirements, and benchmark performance for fine-tuned and finance-specific LLMs. Examples include FinMA trained on roughly 136,000 instruction samples, FinGPT on roughly 50,000 samples, BloombergGPT on hundreds of billions of finance and general-domain tokens, and Fin-T5 on approximately 80 billion finance tokens.

## Methods
The paper is a practical literature survey and synthesis.

It organizes financial LLM approaches into three broad strategies:
1. using existing pretrained LLMs through zero-shot or few-shot prompting,
2. fine-tuning existing models on finance-specific data, and
3. pre-training finance-specific LLMs from scratch.

The authors compare these approaches in terms of data requirements, computational cost, performance, flexibility, privacy, and implementation complexity. They summarize reported benchmark results for financial classification and generative tasks such as sentiment analysis, headline classification, question answering, summarization, and named-entity recognition.

A central contribution is a four-level decision framework for adoption:
- Level 1: zero-shot use,
- Level 2: few-shot prompting,
- Level 3: tool augmentation and/or fine-tuning,
- Level 4: training a finance-specific LLM from scratch.

The framework recommends moving to more expensive forms of customization only when simpler approaches fail to deliver adequate performance.

The paper also distinguishes between conventional task-accuracy metrics such as precision, recall, F1, RMSE, and R² and finance-specific performance metrics such as profit or Sharpe ratio.

## Main findings
The survey concludes that LLMs create substantial opportunities for financial NLP because they can perform useful tasks with little or no labeled data and can adapt to multiple tasks through prompting rather than separate model training.

The authors report that finance-specific fine-tuned LLMs generally outperform their general-purpose base models on finance-domain classification tasks. They also summarize evidence that fine-tuned financial LLMs can outperform powerful general LLMs such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 on some finance classification benchmarks, although performance on generative tasks is more mixed.

Finance-specific models trained from scratch, such as BloombergGPT and Fin-T5, outperform comparable general-domain models on reported financial benchmarks. However, these approaches require substantially greater data and computational resources.

The paper argues that general-purpose LLMs should usually be tried first because zero-shot and few-shot approaches are inexpensive and flexible. Fine-tuning or full domain-specific pre-training should be considered only when the performance gains justify the additional cost and data requirements.

The authors also emphasize that finance-specific model evaluation should not rely only on linguistic accuracy. In applications such as trading, economic performance metrics such as total profit or Sharpe ratio may be more important, and accuracy metrics should align with the ultimate financial objective.

## Relevance to my paper
This paper is useful primarily as a framing and survey reference for the emergence of LLM-based methods in finance rather than as a direct empirical antecedent.

It helps position the methodological progression from traditional supervised models and domain-specific transformers toward general-purpose and finance-specific LLMs. In particular, it provides a taxonomy that distinguishes zero-shot/few-shot prompting, fine-tuning, and training domain-specific LLMs from scratch.

The survey is also relevant because it explicitly notes that LLMs may be valuable when labeled financial data are scarce. This supports the motivation for testing general-purpose LLMs as sentiment measures without requiring large task-specific labeled datasets.

Most importantly for my study, the paper distinguishes between conventional NLP accuracy and application-level financial performance. It states that metrics such as profit or Sharpe ratio can be more important than classification accuracy in finance and that the two should be aligned. This is directly consistent with evaluating sentiment models not only on linguistic benchmarks but also on whether their outputs are economically meaningful.

The paper can also provide background for explaining why more complex models are not automatically preferable. Its decision framework recommends starting with the least costly approach and escalating to fine-tuning or training from scratch only when additional performance justifies the extra resources.

## Caveats / limitations
This is a survey rather than an original empirical study, so its claims about model superiority rely on results reported by other papers rather than on a common controlled benchmark.

Many of the model comparisons are not strictly apples-to-apples because the surveyed systems differ in architecture, training corpora, model size, benchmark datasets, evaluation protocols, and access to proprietary data. Reported performance differences should therefore be interpreted as descriptive rather than as definitive causal evidence that one model class is superior.

The paper was published in 2023, very early in the rapid development of financial LLMs. Its model landscape, cost estimates, proprietary-versus-open-source comparisons, and statements about available applications became dated quickly.

The survey sometimes summarizes benchmark results reported by model developers themselves, which may introduce publication or reporting bias and limits independent comparability.

The discussion of financial evaluation is conceptually useful, but the paper does not itself test whether LLM sentiment measures predict abnormal returns or other realized market outcomes.

Finally, the paper contains some technical imprecision in its background discussion of transformer architectures. For example, it describes GPT as encoder-only and BERT as decoder-only, which reverses the conventional characterization. This does not undermine the survey’s broad taxonomy, but it is a reason to use the paper primarily for high-level framing rather than for detailed technical claims.

Model-list obsolescence: Because this survey was published in 2023, several specific models it discusses (e.g., BLOOM, OPT, early LLaMA/Vicuna/Alpaca-era models, and first-generation finance-specific fine-tunes) are now mainly historical reference points rather than current state-of-the-art systems. The paper is therefore more useful for its taxonomy of LLM adoption strategies—zero/few-shot use, fine-tuning, and domain-specific pre-training—than for its inventory of particular models.
