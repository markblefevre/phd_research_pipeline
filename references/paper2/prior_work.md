Prior research shows that financial text contains information relevant to firm outcomes and market reactions, but extracting that information is not straightforward. Early studies rely on word counts and domain-specific dictionaries, while later work uses supervised machine learning, contextual language models, and generative large language models to capture increasingly complex features of financial language. At the same time, a separate literature shows that economically relevant information is not distributed uniformly throughout long disclosures: some passages are forward-looking, unusual, or substantially changed from prior filings, while others are largely persistent. These two literatures raise a common question about the interaction between information content and measurement technology. Does contextual NLP add more value when financial disclosures contain genuinely new information?
1. Foundations of financial sentiment measurement
Early research establishes that qualitative financial language contains information relevant to investors and market outcomes. Tetlock (2007) shows that pessimistic language in financial news predicts lower short-horizon market returns and higher trading activity, while Tetlock, Saar-Tsechansky, and Macskassy (2008) show that negative language in firm-specific news contains incremental information about subsequent firm fundamentals beyond conventional quantitative measures. Extending this evidence from news to corporate disclosure, Feldman et al. (2010) show that changes in MD&A tone contain incremental information beyond earnings surprises and accruals and are associated with short-window stock returns around the filing date.
These findings raise a second question: how should financial tone be measured? Loughran and McDonald (2011) show that general-purpose sentiment dictionaries can severely misclassify financial language because words that appear negative in ordinary usage often have neutral or specialized meanings in financial contexts. Their finance-specific dictionary produces measures of tone that are more closely related to filing-period market reactions than a general-purpose lexicon. Henry and Leone (2016) reinforce this result, showing that domain-specific wordlists outperform general-purpose dictionaries in explaining market reactions and provide greater statistical power. Importantly, they also find that a simple domain-specific word-frequency measure can perform as well as a more complex Naive Bayes approach in predicting future earnings, cautioning against the assumption that greater methodological complexity necessarily produces a more economically informative measure.
Li (2010) extends this literature by showing that the location of information within a disclosure can matter as much as the method used to measure it. Rather than treating MD&A as a homogeneous text, he first isolates forward-looking statements and then uses a supervised Naive Bayes classifier to measure their tone and content. The resulting measures contain incremental information about future earnings and liquidity beyond conventional predictors. This distinction is important for the present study because it separates two empirical choices: which portions of a long disclosure are most likely to contain economically relevant information, and which technology should be used to measure sentiment within those portions.
Glasserman and Mamaysky (2019) further show that the economic relevance of financial language can depend on whether that language is unusual relative to prior text. Combining sentiment with a measure of textual unusualness produces stronger signals for future market volatility than either dimension considered separately. This result provides an early indication that sentiment and informational novelty may be complementary characteristics of financial text. Taken together, the literature establishes that financial language contains economically meaningful information, but that extracting this information depends both on how sentiment is measured and on where informative language is located within the text.
2. Machine learning, contextual models, and domain adaptation
Machine-learning approaches provide an alternative to fixed sentiment dictionaries by learning how textual features relate to labeled outcomes. Li (2010) provides an early example in financial disclosure, training a supervised Naive Bayes classifier on manually labeled forward-looking MD&A statements to classify their tone and content. Transformer-based models extend this approach substantially by representing words in relation to their surrounding language rather than treating them primarily as independent lexical features.
Araci (2019) introduces FinBERT and shows that a pretrained transformer can outperform earlier machine-learning and neural approaches on labeled financial sentiment tasks, including when relatively little labeled training data are available. This marks an important methodological shift: rather than relying primarily on predetermined financial vocabulary, contextual models attempt to represent how the meaning of language changes with its surrounding text. Huang, Wang, and Yang (2023) subsequently extend the use of large-scale financial-domain pretraining, further developing the case for contextual representations tailored to financial language.
A related literature uses market outcomes directly to learn which textual features are economically relevant. Frankel, Jennings, and Lee (2022) train supervised machine-learning models on short-window abnormal returns and show that these learned measures explain disclosure-period market reactions more consistently than static dictionary measures. Siano (2025) extends this approach using a RoBERTa-based contextual transformer fine-tuned directly on short-window abnormal returns from earnings-announcement disclosures. The resulting text-based measure explains substantially more variation in market reactions than dictionary and conventional n-gram approaches, providing strong evidence that word context contains economically relevant information. Because both approaches use market returns as training targets, however, their measures are better interpreted as supervised text-based predictors of market reaction than as sentiment measures constructed independently of the outcome being explained.
Japanese financial text introduces an additional dimension to the modeling problem. Suzuki et al. (2023) show that adapting both the pretraining corpus and tokenizer vocabulary to Japanese financial language can improve performance on downstream financial NLP tasks. Their tokenizer results are particularly relevant because general Japanese tokenizers may fragment finance-specific terminology into unnatural subword units. The study therefore provides direct evidence that successful contextual modeling of Japanese financial disclosure may require both domain-specific representation and language-specific tokenization. 
This literature highlights an important distinction between learning sentiment from linguistic labels and learning textual signals directly from market outcomes. Li, Araci, and Suzuki construct models using human-assigned or linguistic labels, whereas Frankel and Siano train directly on realized market reactions. The present study follows the former approach: sentiment measures are constructed independently of realized returns and then evaluated against a common market outcome. This separates sentiment measurement from subsequent economic validation.
3. Generative LLMs and financial reasoning
Generative large language models extend contextual financial NLP by allowing financial meaning to be inferred through prompting rather than relying exclusively on task-specific supervised training. This makes them especially attractive when labeled financial data are limited and when the relevant information is complex, dispersed, or difficult to encode through fixed lexical rules. Their greater flexibility and semantic capacity, however, do not by themselves establish that they produce more economically informative sentiment measures.

Fatouros et al. (2023) provide early evidence that a general-purpose LLM can outperform a finance-specific transformer in financial sentiment analysis. Using zero-shot GPT-3.5 on foreign-exchange news headlines, they report substantially higher classification performance than FinBERT and stronger associations between model sentiment and realized market returns. Their results also show that performance is highly sensitive to prompt design and to whether the model is given the relevant financial context. Li et al. (2023) place these developments within a broader framework for financial LLMs, distinguishing zero-shot and few-shot prompting, finance-specific fine-tuning, tool augmentation, and domain-specific pretraining. Their survey also emphasizes that model evaluation in finance should ultimately reflect downstream financial usefulness rather than linguistic accuracy alone.

Chiu and Hung (2025) extend LLM-based sentiment analysis to long financial disclosures by combining a finance-adapted LLaMA-2 model with a two-stage summarization pipeline for MD&A text. Their LLaMA-based sentiment measures outperform FinBERT, dictionary methods, and conventional machine-learning approaches in several return-based tests, although the design makes it difficult to separate the contribution of the language model from that of the summarization procedure. Lopez-Lira and Tang (2026) provide broader evidence on model capability, comparing GPT-family models with BERT-based methods, FinBERT, Llama variants, RavenPack, and dictionary measures. They find that GPT-4 is especially effective at interpreting the short-term economic implications of firm-specific news and that its classifications are associated with both immediate market reactions and subsequent return drift. Together, these studies suggest that more sophisticated language models can extract economically relevant information missed by simpler approaches, but that measured performance depends importantly on model design, input construction, and the economic task used for evaluation.

The evidence therefore suggests that generative and highly contextual models can extract economically relevant information missed by simpler approaches, but their advantage is not universal. The unresolved question is whether their additional contextual capacity yields incrementally more market-relevant sentiment when competing methods are applied to the same disclosure text under a common empirical design.

4. Japanese financial text and closest empirical precedents
The Japanese setting introduces linguistic and institutional considerations that distinguish it from much of the English-language financial NLP literature. Japanese financial text requires language-specific tokenization and can benefit from domain-adapted language models, while annual securities reports are produced under a disclosure regime and document structure distinct from U.S. corporate filings. Recent Japanese research now spans both supervised NLP evaluation and economic validation against firm fundamentals and market outcomes. The relevant question is therefore no longer whether financial sentiment can be measured in Japanese regulatory text, but whether the relative usefulness of alternative measurement technologies depends on the information contained in the disclosure.
Kato and Goto (2021) provide a particularly important antecedent. Using MD&A-related text from Japanese annual securities reports obtained through EDINET, they measure tone with a Japanese translation of the Loughran–McDonald financial sentiment dictionary. Building on Li (2010), they do not isolate explicitly forward-looking sentences. Instead, they remove the component of full-MD&A tone explained by contemporaneously observable firm characteristics and interpret the residual as a proxy for managers’ qualitative future outlook. This adjusted tone is positively associated with subsequent operating performance. Their results provide evidence that a transparent lexical measure applied to Japanese regulatory disclosure can contain information about future firm performance, while also showing that the informativeness of disclosure may depend on which component of the text is being measured.
Suzuki et al. (2023) provide an important methodological foundation for contextual modeling in Japanese finance. They show that adapting both the pretraining corpus and tokenizer vocabulary to financial language generally improves performance on Japanese financial NLP tasks and demonstrate a concrete role for language-specific tokenization, particularly for financial terminology that may be fragmented by general-purpose tokenizers. The benefits of adaptation are nevertheless task-dependent, and the study evaluates models primarily against supervised NLP benchmarks rather than realized market outcomes. It therefore establishes the importance of Japanese- and finance-specific model construction without resolving whether improved linguistic performance translates into greater economic informativeness.
Okada et al. (2025) provide one of the closest empirical precedents to the present study. Using Japanese annual securities reports, they compare dictionary-based sentiment, a chABSA-fine-tuned DeBERTaV2 model, and general-purpose generative LLMs including GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3 Haiku, and Gemini 1.5 Flash. GPT-4 and Claude sentiment measures are significantly associated with subsequent abnormal returns, whereas the dictionary measures and DeBERTaV2 do not produce significant risk-adjusted performance. Importantly, the relation is contrarian: firms with more positive LLM sentiment subsequently underperform those with more negative sentiment. These results show that greater linguistic sophistication can reveal economically relevant information not captured by simpler measures, while also demonstrating that economic informativeness need not take the form of a straightforward positive relation between sentiment and subsequent returns.
These studies substantially narrow the methodological gap in Japanese financial NLP. Prior evidence shows that annual-report tone can contain information about future firm performance, that domain adaptation can improve contextual NLP performance, and that generative LLM measures can differ materially from lexical and transformer-based measures when evaluated against market outcomes. What remains less understood is how the informational characteristics of the underlying disclosure affect these relative differences.
5. Information location and forward-looking disclosure
A separate literature shows that the economic usefulness of financial text depends not only on how language is measured, but also on which portions of a disclosure are analyzed. Long corporate reports combine descriptions of past performance, current conditions, risk factors, boilerplate, and forward-looking discussion, and these components need not contain equal amounts of economically relevant information. Studies that isolate particular categories of disclosure therefore challenge the assumption that sentiment measured over an entire document provides the most informative representation of its content.
Li (2010) provides an early example of economically motivated text selection. He first identifies explicit forward-looking statements within MD&A and then classifies their tone using a supervised Naive Bayes model. The resulting forward-looking tone contains incremental information about future profitability and liquidity and is not fully incorporated into analysts’ forecasts. The study therefore establishes that identifying a potentially informative subset of disclosure can be an important step before measuring its sentiment.
Muslu et al. (2015) examine a different dimension of forward-looking disclosure: its quantity rather than its tone. Using a rule-based procedure to identify forward-looking sentences in U.S. 10-K MD&A, they find that firms with weaker pre-filing information environments provide more abnormal forward-looking disclosure and that filing-period returns subsequently incorporate future earnings information more strongly. The effect is concentrated in operations-related and relatively short-horizon forward-looking statements, indicating that even within prospective disclosure, economic relevance varies across content and horizon. Their design also highlights an important distinction between identifying where potentially useful information resides and determining how sentiment within that information should be measured.
Kato and Goto (2021) provide a complementary Japanese approach. Rather than identifying explicit forward-looking sentences, they infer managers’ future outlook from the component of full-MD&A tone unexplained by contemporaneously observable firm characteristics. Together with Li (2010) and Muslu et al. (2015), their study illustrates that potentially informative disclosure can be isolated in different ways rather than treating the full document as a homogeneous information set.
This literature establishes that economically relevant information is unevenly distributed within financial disclosures, but forward-lookingness and informational novelty are distinct concepts. A statement may concern future conditions while closely resembling language used in a prior filing, and highly similar text may still contain economically important updates such as revised numerical forecasts. Conversely, newly introduced language may concern past, current, or future developments. This distinction motivates a separate examination of textual novelty as a complementary dimension of disclosure information rather than as a synonym for forward-looking or nonpersistent text.
6. Textual change, novelty, and disclosure informativeness
A related literature examines whether changes in disclosure language reveal information that is difficult to identify from the current filing in isolation. This work treats disclosure as a dynamic object rather than a static text: recurring language may reflect persistent firm conditions, reporting conventions, or regulatory requirements, while changes in wording can signal new developments, revised expectations, or shifts in managerial emphasis. Textual novelty therefore adds a temporal dimension to disclosure analysis by measuring how much the current report differs from what the firm communicated previously.
Brown and Tucker (2011) provide early large-sample evidence that year-over-year changes in MD&A are economically meaningful. Using TF-IDF and cosine similarity to compare current and prior-year MD&A, they show that firms experiencing greater underlying economic change modify their disclosures more substantially and that greater textual modification is associated with a larger absolute stock-price reaction around the 10-K filing. Their results establish an important precedent for using changes in disclosure language, rather than textual levels alone, as a proxy for newly disclosed information.
Dyer, Lang, and Stice-Lawrence (2017) document why such temporal comparisons may be particularly important in long corporate reports. They show that U.S. 10-Ks became substantially longer, more redundant, more boilerplate, and more persistent over time, with much of the increase concentrated in regulatory-compliance topics. Their evidence does not directly establish that repeated language is economically uninformative, but it demonstrates that a large portion of annual-report text can reflect persistent disclosure structure rather than newly introduced content. This creates a natural rationale for distinguishing textual sentiment from textual novelty.
Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen (2020) provide stronger evidence that year-over-year textual change itself contains economically relevant information. Comparing successive 10-K and 10-Q filings using several similarity measures, they show that larger disclosure changes predict subsequent operating deterioration and lower future stock returns, while prices exhibit little immediate reaction when the filings are released. They interpret this pattern as evidence that investors underreact to information embedded in subtle textual changes. Importantly, they also show that textual change and sentiment are distinct constructs: the extent of change remains informative, while the sentiment of the changed language provides additional information about the direction of subsequent outcomes.
An important qualification follows from Muslu et al. (2015): textual persistence should not be equated mechanically with stale or uninformative disclosure. Highly similar language can still contain economically important updates, such as changes in numerical forecasts or expectations. Novelty is therefore better viewed as a continuous characteristic of disclosure than as a simple division between informative new text and uninformative repeated text. This consideration motivates careful treatment of numerical changes and robustness analyses around alternative definitions of textual similarity.
The novelty literature complements the forward-looking-disclosure literature by identifying a different dimension of information content. Forward-lookingness describes the temporal orientation of language, whereas novelty captures the extent to which the current disclosure departs from prior communication. Existing studies, however, generally examine textual change itself rather than whether novelty affects the relative usefulness of alternative sentiment-measurement technologies. This leaves open whether contextual and generative models provide their greatest incremental value when applied to disclosure language that is more novel relative to the firm’s prior report.
7. Research gap
Prior research establishes three related findings. First, financial sentiment can be measured using technologies ranging from domain-specific dictionaries to contextual transformers and generative LLMs. Second, the economic usefulness of textual information depends on which portions of a disclosure are analyzed. Third, year-over-year textual change can reveal information that is difficult to identify from a filing considered in isolation. Although these literatures increasingly overlap, they have not resolved how measurement technology and informational novelty interact.
Recent work substantially narrows the methodological gap. In particular, studies such as Okada et al. (2025) already compare lexical, transformer-based, and generative sentiment measures using Japanese annual securities reports and market-based outcomes. The remaining question is therefore not whether advanced language models can be applied to Japanese financial disclosure or whether they can outperform simpler measures in some settings. Rather, it is whether their relative advantage depends systematically on the informational characteristics of the text being analyzed.
The present study focuses on textual novelty as that conditioning dimension. If contextual and generative models derive their advantage from interpreting compositional meaning, subtle changes in phrasing, and interactions among words, their incremental value may be greatest when disclosure language departs meaningfully from prior communication. Conversely, when disclosure text is highly persistent, a specialized lexical measure may capture much of the relevant sentiment with less model complexity. This leads to the central research question: Does contextual NLP add more value when financial disclosures contain genuinely new information?
The study therefore compares lexical, contextual, and generative sentiment measures under a common economic-validation framework and examines whether their relative association with market reactions varies with textual novelty. This design shifts the comparison from a simple model horse race toward a conditional question: not merely which sentiment technology performs best, but under what informational conditions greater contextual sophistication becomes economically useful.
