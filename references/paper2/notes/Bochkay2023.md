## Research question
What does the paper ask?

How has textual analysis been used in leading accounting research, what are the available NLP methods for common accounting tasks, and how should researchers choose, implement, and **validate** text-derived measures?

The survey addresses several connected questions:

- How should NLP approaches be organized by **text representation**, **model**, and **output construct**?
- When are simple dictionaries or bag-of-words (BOW) comparisons adequate, and when might embeddings, machine learning, or deep learning improve measurement?
- How can researchers assess the **construct validity** of sentiment, similarity, forward-lookingness, and other variables extracted from text?
- Which implementation decisions (preprocessing, annotations, model choice, evaluation metrics) materially affect research conclusions?
- What research questions become feasible when more sophisticated NLP methods scale up human analysis?

This is a **methodological literature survey**, not a paper testing whether sentiment or disclosure novelty predicts security returns.

## Data

### Survey corpus
The authors identify **207 studies** employing textual analysis in leading accounting journals between **2010 and 2021** (pp. 766, 795; PDF pp. 2, 31).

The journal universe comprises *The Accounting Review*, *Journal of Accounting Research*, *Journal of Accounting and Economics*, *Contemporary Accounting Research*, *Review of Accounting Studies*, *Accounting, Organizations, and Society*, and the accounting track of *Management Science*.

The selection procedure involved reviewing abstracts and research discussions, screening remaining articles with textual-analysis keywords, and confirming included articles through review. A research assistant supplied preliminary classifications, one coauthor systematically categorized the papers, and another reviewed some classifications (p. 795, PDF p. 31, Appendix 2).

### What is being measured
The authors code the **role** and **task** of NLP in prior accounting studies; the unit of analysis for their descriptive evidence is the *published study*, not the filing or sentence.

Among the 207 papers, **52.7%** use text-derived variables directly for hypothesis testing and **29.9%** extract targeted information to construct variables for subsequent analysis (p. 766, PDF p. 2). Task shares include **37.2%** measuring sentiment, **25.6%** readability, **10.1%** disclosure similarity, and **6.8%** forward-looking information (p. 777, PDF p. 13; Figure 3, p. 797, PDF p. 33). These tasks can overlap within a study.

The article also synthesizes published numerical evidence from other studies, such as FinBERT sentiment accuracy. **Those are results of the cited studies, not new estimates generated by Bochkay et al.**

## Methods

### Systematic but interpretive review
The paper organizes its 207-study survey into applications and methodological categories. It is a review and conceptual synthesis, not a new NLP model, new training dataset, or market-return regression.

### Three-part framework for NLP
The authors distinguish (1) how text is represented, (2) the NLP model transforming the input, and (3) the output or construct to be measured (pp. 767, 769–770; PDF pp. 3, 5–6). Table 1 (p. 770, PDF p. 6) crosses BOW/word embeddings with simple transformations, text comparisons, traditional machine learning, and deep learning.

- **Bag of words / TF–IDF:** representation based on word occurrences or weighted frequencies; useful when exact wording is substantively important.
- **Embedding representations:** capture semantic relatedness and can distinguish word overlap from meaning overlap. The survey discusses word and document embeddings; it does not evaluate modern Japanese sentence embeddings such as Ruri.
- **Comparison methods:** vector-space cosine similarity can quantify textual persistence or modification. Cosine similarity can be applied to BOW or embedding vectors; the resulting meaning differs depending on the representation.
- **Classification methods:** dictionaries, Naïve Bayes, other supervised machine-learning models, and deep learning can each support textual classification under different assumptions.

### Applications especially relevant to this study
**Sentiment (Section 3.1, pp. 777–779; PDF pp. 13–15).** Dictionary methods are transparent but neglect context, differential sentiment intensity, negation, and domain-specific usage. Supervised and contextual models can address some of these limitations, although gains must be demonstrated for the task at hand.

**Disclosure similarity (Section 3.3, pp. 780–781; PDF pp. 16–17).** The survey identifies Brown and Tucker (2011) as a prominent year-over-year MD&A similarity application. BOW-based similarity measures literal lexical overlap; embedding-based similarity can identify semantically similar wording. The paper gives examples in which statements with different word choices convey the same economic meaning.

**Forward-looking content (Section 3.4, pp. 781–782; PDF pp. 17–18).** A task-specific sentence classification can provide more relevant textual measures than a crude dictionary flag. The survey discusses Li (2010), Muslu et al. (2015), and supervised approaches, illustrating that document subsets and categories matter.

### Construct-validation framework
Section 4 (pp. 782–785; PDF pp. 18–21) makes construct validity central. The authors separate:

- **Face and content validity:** does the measure genuinely represent the construct as defined? Content validity ideally uses direct comparisons with independently evaluated text.
- **Convergent, concurrent, predictive, and discriminant validity:** does the measure behave as theory predicts relative to other measures or outcomes? These are indirect forms of validation.

They recommend, when feasible, using independent human coders to assess text-derived categories. For supervised ML, they discuss training/validation/test splits, per-class precision/recall/F1, class balance, annotation quality, and inter-annotator agreement. They advise **at least two human annotators** and describe an approximately **80% agreement heuristic for binary classifications**, explicitly noting that lower agreement can be expected for multiclass tasks (pp. 783–785; PDF pp. 19–21).

The paper also warns about **indirect annotations**: labeling textual sentiment using financial outcomes such as earnings changes or stock returns may produce a predictor of the chosen outcome without cleanly measuring *linguistic sentiment* (p. 785; PDF p. 21). It discusses Frankel et al. (2021 working-paper version) and Siano and Wysocki (2021) as examples; this is **not** an assessment of the later Siano (2025) publication.

### Implementation and model selection
Section 5 (pp. 786–789; PDF pp. 22–25) covers scraping/extraction, text cleaning, tokenization, stop words, stemming, model selection, and annotated samples. It explicitly cautions that stop-word removal and morphological normalization are not universally desirable, particularly for semantic models whose inputs retain context. Model selection should consider **functionality, simplicity, familiarity, performance, and computing costs** (p. 787, PDF p. 23).

## Main findings

1. **Textual analysis became a mainstream accounting method.** The annual volume of surveyed studies rose markedly over 2010–2021; the authors document 207 relevant publications, concentrated in corporate disclosures (pp. 766, 795–797; PDF pp. 2, 31–33).

2. **The field historically relied heavily on dictionaries.** Of the surveyed papers that measure sentiment, **97.4%** used a dictionary method (p. 777, PDF p. 13). This is a description of the 2010–2021 literature, not the current 2026 field.

3. **Different text representations answer different questions.** Exact-word overlap can be appropriate when the research construct is literal similarity; embeddings can be more appropriate for semantic equivalence. Neither is automatically superior without specifying the target construct (pp. 780–781, PDF pp. 16–17).

4. **NLP advances make richer measurement feasible.** The authors summarize evidence from prior classification studies in which contextual models outperform dictionaries and some conventional classifiers, while also offering ways to analyze linguistic constructs at sentence level (pp. 778–779, PDF pp. 14–15).

5. **Construct validity is not established by predictive performance alone.** A text variable can correlate with a market outcome yet fail to measure the construct claimed for it. The authors treat independent annotations, transparent definitions, and appropriate validation tests as central research-design concerns (pp. 782–785, PDF pp. 18–21).

6. **Sophistication is not an end in itself.** Researchers should choose tools that match the economic question and be able to explain how those tools construct the measures used in econometric tests (pp. 787, 791–792; PDF pp. 23, 27–28).

**Interpretive note:** The article's numbers describe its *survey sample*. It does not report a return-prediction result, market reaction to novelty, or validated classification of unchanged/revised/new MD&A text.

## Relevance to my paper

This is a **high-priority methodological and editorial reference** for Paper 2, which analyzes the sentiment of unchanged, revised, and newly introduced passages in consecutive Japanese MD&A disclosures.

**1. It supplies the methodological bridge from whole-document similarity to semantic sentence matching.** My whole-document TF–IDF cosine novelty resembles the conventional BOW comparison reviewed in Section 3.3. My Ruri retrieval, lexical reranking, and GPT-based proposition classification pursue a different goal: determine whether current-year statements are substantively equivalent to, revise, or have no counterpart in the prior MD&A. The survey helps explain why lexical overlap alone cannot consistently answer this question.

**2. It helps sharpen the *construct* being measured.** My three labels are defined by **relationship to the prior annual MD&A**. They do not directly establish that the proposition was unknown to investors, economically material, or causally responsible for filing-window returns. This is exactly the kind of construct-versus-proxy distinction the authors emphasize. It supports the careful wording already used in Sections 2.5, 4.3, and the conclusion of Paper 2.

**3. It strongly supports an independent annotation-validation exercise.** My current LLM assigns five categories on 3,463,989 sentences, but **independent human-annotation validation remains outstanding**, as disclosed in Paper 2. The survey offers a concrete rationale for stratified expert review, at least two annotators, reported class-specific confusion matrices/precision/recall, and annotation agreement. The boundary between *revised* and *new*, including changed numbers and false retrieval negatives, deserves special attention. The survey does not itself validate my labels.

**4. It clarifies why market-return associations are not sufficient evidence of measurement validity.** My component sentiment scores are constructed independently of realized stock returns, then evaluated through CAR regressions. This avoids *directly labeling sentiment from event returns*, but it does not by itself guarantee correct sentiment scoring or correct disclosure-change categories. Similarly, a statistically significant CAR coefficient does not establish content validity or causality.

**5. It helps interpret the independently constructed binary robustness classifier.** The fact that my binary persistent/novel results differ from the five-category model is evidence of sensitivity to how the construct is operationalized, not a direct validation of the latter classifier. Bochkay et al. provide vocabulary for presenting this distinction.

**6. It reinforces the distinction between computational novelty and economic contribution.** The paper endorses modern NLP when needed for a well-defined research question but cautions against making the tool itself the primary claim. My contribution should therefore be framed as asking whether sentiment's association with filing-window CAR varies by *where text lies in the evolution of disclosure*, not merely as combining Ruri with an LLM.

**7. It provides useful context for the Japanese setting.** The review is dominated by existing accounting studies, mostly English-language corporate disclosures. It offers methodological foundations but no Japanese tokenizer validation or evidence that a model trained on English text transfers to Japanese EDINET filings. My cited Japanese NLP and disclosure papers remain necessary.

**8. It does not establish that my sentence-to-sentence alignment is unprecedented.** Prior work already used embedding-based text comparisons. A claim of methodological novelty must be restricted to the specific longitudinal classification, sentiment decomposition, institutional setting, and empirical design, and verified against more recent research not covered by this 2010–2021 survey.

## How I might cite it

- Bochkay et al. (2023) survey textual analysis in leading accounting journals and organize methods around text representation, the NLP model, and the resulting empirical construct.
- Their review distinguishes the role of BOW similarity for lexical overlap from embedding-based methods for semantic similarity, motivating a semantic comparison of consecutive MD&A sentences.
- The authors identify disclosure similarity and sentiment measurement as distinct NLP tasks; my study combines these dimensions by assigning sentiment to specific categories of disclosure change.
- They emphasize that increasingly sophisticated textual models must be evaluated by whether the resulting measures capture the intended underlying construct.
- Their discussion of independent human annotation provides a clear rationale for validating my unchanged/revised/new labels rather than equating consistent LLM execution with classification accuracy.
- The survey cautions that financial outcomes used as *indirect sentiment labels* may make the measured construct ambiguous; my sentiment scoring is performed independently of realized stock returns.
- It documents a broader methodological trend from dictionaries and BOW toward contextual models while cautioning researchers to select the simplest method that adequately serves the research question.
- It supports describing the binary-classification robustness test as an alternative operationalization, not proof that the primary five-category classifier is correct.

## Caveats / limitations

**Nature of evidence.** This is a survey of prior studies, not an original test showing that embedding-based similarity, LLM classification, or sentiment predicts returns in my Japanese setting. When reporting numerical model comparisons, attribute the estimates to the *underlying* studies, not to experiments newly conducted by Bochkay et al.

**Coverage period.** Its empirical review ends in **2021**, even though the paper appeared in **2023**. It therefore cannot establish the status of the literature in 2026 or the originality of methods introduced after 2021.

**Geographic and linguistic applicability.** The surveyed literature is primarily based on English-language accounting/financial disclosures. The discussion cannot replace validation of Japanese sentence segmentation, embeddings, or financial-language interpretation.

**Word embeddings versus sentence embeddings.** Much of the survey concerns Word2Vec, Doc2Vec, and contextual word representations. Ruri is a sentence-embedding model. The lexical-versus-semantic comparison is conceptually relevant, but the paper does **not** provide direct performance evidence for Ruri-v3-310m or my retrieval/reranking pipeline.

**Not a study of modern generative LLM prompting.** The review predates mainstream instruction-tuned GPT-style classification at scale. Its training/annotation recommendations are valuable, but the specifics of a fixed-prompt, pretrained generative classifier differ from training a supervised model on researcher labels.

**Construct validity is still untested for my core classification.** The survey actually raises the standard against which my current framework should be judged. Running a consistent prompt on millions of sentences establishes coverage and reproducibility, not independent human agreement or correct classification.

**Annotation agreement is not a one-size-fits-all requirement.** The paper's 80% heuristic concerns *binary* classification. My five-category task includes difficult and imbalanced labels, so design and report agreement thresholds appropriate to each category instead of simply copying 80%.

**Market correlation is not proof of linguistic validity.** A text-derived variable can be associated with CAR for reasons beyond the theoretical construct, including concurrent disclosures and prior information availability. Independent return-free sentiment construction avoids one indirect-label problem but does not solve all construct-validity issues.

**Different measured constructs can yield different results.** The paper's taxonomy reinforces that BOW novelty, embedding retrieval, and LLM proposition labels capture related but nonidentical properties. The five-category and binary robustness results should be understood as sensitivity to definitions, not as fully comparable estimates.

**Authorship/citation bookkeeping.** The official year is 2023; do not mistakenly cite 2022 because of the filename/copyright line. Use my existing BibTeX key `bochkay2023textual`; preserve *Contemporary Accounting Research* capitalization.
