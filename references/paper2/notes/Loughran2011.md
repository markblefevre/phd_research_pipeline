## Research question
What does the paper ask?

Do general-purpose sentiment dictionaries accurately measure tone in financial disclosures, or do they misclassify common financial terminology? More specifically, Loughran and McDonald ask whether the widely used Harvard Psychosociological Dictionary is appropriate for 10-K text, whether a finance-specific dictionary provides a cleaner measure of tone, and whether alternative financial word categories are associated with economically relevant outcomes such as filing-date returns, trading volume, future volatility, fraud, material weaknesses, and unexpected earnings.

## Data
The main sample contains 50,115 firm-year 10-K filings from 8,341 unique U.S. firms over 1994–2008.

The authors begin with 121,217 10-K and 10-K405 filings downloaded from EDGAR and apply filters requiring a CRSP match, ordinary common equity, market-capitalization data, a stock price above $3, sufficient return and volume history, NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq listing, positive book value, and a minimum 10-K length of 2,000 words.

A separate MD&A sample contains 37,287 firm-year observations for which the MD&A section can be identified and contains at least 250 words.

The full 10-K corpus contains approximately 2.5 billion words.

The paper also uses:
- CRSP returns and trading-volume data,
- Compustat accounting data,
- institutional-ownership data,
- analyst forecast dispersion and revisions,
- a sample of firms accused of accounting fraud in Rule 10b-5 class actions, and
- a sample of firms subsequently reporting material weaknesses in internal controls.

For preliminary out-of-sample context checks, the authors also examine seasoned equity offering prospectuses and Dow Jones news stories.

## Methods
The paper uses a bag-of-words approach to parse 10-K filings into word counts after excluding tables and exhibits.

The principal comparison is between:
- **H4N-Inf:** an inflected version of the Harvard-IV-4 General Inquirer negative-word list, and
- **Fin-Neg:** a new finance-specific negative-word list developed by the authors.

The original Harvard list contains 2,005 negative root words; after adding appropriate inflections, H4N-Inf contains 4,187 words. The authors show that many high-frequency Harvard “negative” words—such as *tax*, *cost*, *capital*, *board*, *liability*, and *foreign*—are often neutral in financial documents.

To construct the finance-specific dictionary, the authors examine words occurring in at least 5% of the 10-K corpus and classify them according to their typical financial usage. Fin-Neg contains 2,337 words, about half of which overlap with H4N-Inf. Examples unique to Fin-Neg include *restated*, *litigation*, *termination*, *penalties*, *misstatement*, *misconduct*, *noncompliance*, *deterioration*, and *felony*.

They also create five additional finance-specific word lists:
- positive (Fin-Pos),
- uncertainty (Fin-Unc),
- litigiousness (Fin-Lit),
- strong modal words (MW-Strong), and
- weak modal words (MW-Weak).

Simple negation is handled for positive words when one of six negation terms occurs within three words before a positive term.

The authors compare two ways of aggregating word counts:
1. simple proportional frequency, and
2. **tf.idf-style term weighting**, which dampens the influence of very frequent words and gives relatively more weight to rarer, potentially more informative terms.

Economic validation centers on a four-day filing-event return, days [0,3], calculated as the firm's buy-and-hold return minus the CRSP value-weighted market return.

They estimate quarterly Fama-MacBeth regressions with Newey-West standard errors and controls including firm size, book-to-market, turnover, pre-filing Fama-French alpha, institutional ownership, Nasdaq listing, and industry effects.

The paper also tests relationships with abnormal trading volume, subsequent return volatility, standardized unexpected earnings, accounting-fraud allegations, and subsequent material-weakness disclosures.

Finally, the authors test whether negative tone predicts one-year future returns using long-short portfolios sorted on negative-word measures.

## Main findings
The central finding is that general-purpose dictionaries can severely misclassify financial language.

Approximately **73.8% of the negative-word counts generated by the Harvard dictionary come from words that are typically not negative in a financial context**. Frequent examples include *tax*, *cost*, *capital*, *board*, *liability*, and *foreign*. These misclassifications can add measurement noise and, in some cases, unintentionally proxy for industries or firm characteristics.

Using simple proportional word counts, the Harvard negative-word measure shows little systematic relation with filing-date returns, whereas the finance-specific Fin-Neg measure produces a clear monotonic pattern: firms with more Fin-Neg words experience more negative four-day filing returns.

In multivariate regressions using proportional frequencies, Fin-Neg is significantly negatively related to filing-date excess returns, while H4N-Inf is not. However, once tf.idf-style term weighting is applied, both negative-word lists perform similarly because weighting reduces the influence of high-frequency misclassified words.

The additional finance-specific dictionaries also contain economically relevant information. Negative, uncertainty, strong-modal, and weak-modal word measures are significantly related to filing-period returns under proportional weighting; term weighting generally increases the significance of the various word lists.

Negative-word measures are associated with greater abnormal trading volume around the filing date, and most of the word-list measures are positively associated with subsequent stock-return volatility.

Some word categories also predict economically meaningful firm outcomes. Fin-Neg and strong-modal language are associated with subsequent disclosures of material weaknesses, and term-weighted word measures show relationships with fraud and accounting-control problems.

The authors find that the MD&A section does **not** provide a clearly superior tone signal relative to the full 10-K. Results based only on MD&A are weaker, and the MD&A sample itself is nonrandom because many firms—especially earlier in the sample and larger firms—incorporate the section by reference.

The paper does **not** find evidence that negative-word measures generate significant one-year long-short abnormal returns. Thus, the dictionary measures appear useful for measuring contemporaneous tone and other financial outcomes but do not provide a robust standalone trading signal over the subsequent year.

## Relevance to my paper
This is the foundational benchmark for dictionary-based financial sentiment analysis and is central to my study.

The paper establishes the key principle that **sentiment dictionaries must be domain appropriate**. A word can have a negative meaning in ordinary English but be neutral in financial reporting. The title itself illustrates the problem: *liability* is categorized as negative by a general dictionary even though it is often simply an accounting term.

This is especially relevant to Japanese financial sentiment analysis. The broader lesson is not merely that the Loughran-McDonald dictionary is useful in English, but that linguistic resources developed for one domain—or one language—cannot be assumed to transfer cleanly to another. A Japanese finance-specific dictionary should therefore be treated as a methodological analogue to Loughran-McDonald rather than as a direct translation of a general sentiment lexicon.

The paper also provides an important early example of **economic validation**. The authors do not validate their dictionary solely through linguistic judgment; they test whether the resulting textual measures are associated with filing-date returns, trading volume, volatility, earnings outcomes, fraud, and material weaknesses.

This makes Loughran and McDonald an important benchmark for comparing dictionary methods with later machine-learning, transformer, and LLM methods. More sophisticated methods should ideally demonstrate incremental economic information relative to a well-designed domain-specific dictionary, not merely improved linguistic complexity.

The tf.idf results are also important. They show that some apparent dictionary weakness comes from the weighting scheme rather than the dictionary alone: downweighting extremely common words substantially mitigates the noise from misclassification. This provides useful precedent for treating text representation and term weighting as separate methodological choices from sentiment classification itself.

Finally, the paper's failure to find significant one-year return predictability is useful for distinguishing **contemporaneous economic relevance** from **long-horizon forecasting ability**. A sentiment measure can be meaningfully related to filing-day market reactions and firm characteristics without constituting a profitable standalone trading signal.

- General-purpose sentiment dictionaries can severely misclassify words when applied to financial text.
- Loughran and McDonald show that nearly three-fourths of Harvard-dictionary negative-word occurrences in 10-Ks are not typically negative in a financial context.
- Domain-specific sentiment dictionaries can reduce measurement error and improve the economic relevance of textual tone measures.
- The Loughran-McDonald financial dictionary provides a foundational benchmark for dictionary-based financial sentiment analysis.
- Financial negative-word frequencies are associated with filing-date market reactions, trading volume, and subsequent volatility.
- Finance-specific uncertainty, litigiousness, and modal-word categories provide additional dimensions of financial textual information beyond simple positive/negative tone.

## Caveats / limitations
The approach is fundamentally bag-of-words. It ignores word order, syntax, sentence structure, broader context, and most forms of compositional meaning. A finance-specific dictionary reduces lexical misclassification but does not solve the contextual limitations of word-count methods.

Negation handling is extremely limited. The authors apply simple negation only to positive words and only when one of six negation terms occurs within three words before the positive term. More complex constructions, long-distance negation, conditional language, and contextual reversals are not captured.

The finance-specific dictionaries are constructed from U.S. English-language 10-Ks from 1994–2008. Their vocabulary reflects the accounting, regulatory, and linguistic conventions of that setting and period. Direct transfer to other languages, jurisdictions, document types, or more recent financial text is not guaranteed.

The authors explicitly acknowledge that tone may not causally affect returns. Textual tone may instead proxy for contemporaneous accounting or other fundamental information contained in the filing. The paper therefore establishes association and measurement usefulness rather than a clean causal channel.

The MD&A subsample is nonrandom. Many firms, particularly larger firms and firms earlier in the sample, incorporate MD&A by reference, making the section difficult to parse. This complicates comparisons between full-document and MD&A-specific results.

The Harvard dictionary comparison is an especially weak benchmark for finance because it was designed for psychology and sociology. Demonstrating that a finance-specific dictionary outperforms such a general lexicon does not establish that dictionary methods are optimal relative to later supervised or contextual NLP models.

Term weighting substantially narrows the performance gap between the Harvard and Fin-Neg lists. This means part of the improvement attributed to domain-specific vocabulary depends on the chosen aggregation method, and vocabulary design should not be evaluated independently of representation and weighting.

The paper finds no significant one-year abnormal return predictability from negative-word portfolios. Its strongest evidence concerns contemporaneous filing reactions and associations with other financial outcomes, not a persistent investment anomaly.

Finally, although the paper introduces multiple finance-specific word categories, substantial overlap exists among negative, uncertainty, and litigious word lists. The authors therefore caution that the additional categories should be used primarily when motivated by a specific research question rather than interpreted as fully independent linguistic constructs.
